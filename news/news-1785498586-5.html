Oil Prices Resist $100 Despite Middle East Supply Threats

Wed, 29 Jul 2026 07:06

Tensions are rising in the international crude oil market as the United States and Iran have resumed hostilities. Traffic through the Strait of Hormuz has sharply declined again, and the Houthi rebels are threatening Saudi Arabia’s oil shipments via the Red Sea, making the situation more challenging than before. Fortunately, a temporary ceasefire between the two countries has led to a drop in oil prices, but the oil industry anticipates that if both the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea are blocked, the global crude oil market could face severe supply shortages.

Although the war has resumed, international oil prices have not reached the peak levels seen after the outbreak of the Middle East war in February. This is an unusual phenomenon given the expanded frontlines and worsening conditions. Experts attribute this to a significant reduction in global oil demand, which has outpaced supply declines as countries worldwide respond to the crisis. Notably, they suspect that China’s massive release of strategic oil reserves is a major driver of this demand reduction, as reported by Yahoo Finance and other international media.

Global daily crude oil consumption is estimated at around 100 million barrels. Since the U.S. and Israel attacked Iran in February, Iran’s oil exports have been blocked, reducing daily supply by approximately 11 million barrels—roughly 11% of global demand. To compensate for this shortfall, led by the U.S., countries worldwide have released strategic reserves, causing global reserve levels to drop to historic lows.

With the war’s resumption, the Houthi rebels have declared they will target ships passing through the Red Sea to disrupt Saudi Arabia’s oil exports, which amount to 5 million barrels per day. Combined with the reduced traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, approximately 16% of global demand is now under threat. Despite this, oil futures prices have not reached the levels seen during the initial U.S.-Iran clashes. In April and May, U.S. WTI (West Texas Intermediate) exceeded $92 per barrel, and Brent crude surpassed $101. Given the heightened supply risks, prices might have been expected to surge past $100 in the short term—but they have not. Why haven’t international oil prices risen as sharply as before? Will this trend continue?

Natasha Kanerva, a JP Morgan investment strategist, explained, “The market is adjusting due to a significant drop in demand.” While an initial surge in oil hoarding was expected after the war began, the current situation shows the opposite: global demand has decreased in response to reduced supply. She noted that if demand had not fallen, oil prices would have risen much faster due to supply shortages caused by the war.

Global crude oil demand is now declining faster than at any time in the past six years, except during the 2020 COVID-19 crisis. The International Energy Agency (IEA) projected in July that global oil demand would decrease by 1 million barrels per day this year compared to 2023—a reduction twice as large as its May forecast.

Where has the global oil market’s demand reduction originated? The world economy grew above its potential rate in the first half of this year, making it unlikely that oil consumption dropped significantly. In this context, experts point to reduced Chinese purchasing as the primary cause. As the world’s largest oil importer, China is believed to be responding to the Middle East conflict by cutting imports and instead using its strategic reserves, which are not well reflected in official statistics. OECD countries’ oil reserve data is already poor, and other nations are even less transparent. Some experts suspect that China is not properly disclosing its reserve levels.

However, attributing the subdued oil prices solely to China may not be appropriate. Since the U.S.-Iran war began, Southeast Asian countries have reduced operational days, European airlines have cut flight frequencies, and the U.S. has released its largest-ever strategic reserves to suppress price increases.

Yet experts argue that the recent demand decline, coinciding with the war’s resumption, has occurred too rapidly and massively to be explained by these factors alone. The current drop in oil demand is twice as large as during the 2008 global financial crisis. To solve this mystery, experts are scrutinizing China’s oil statistics.
