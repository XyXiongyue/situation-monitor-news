Global shipping in 2026: falling demand, elevated rates, and rising chokepoint risks

2026-08-25 18:26

inInternational Shipping News25/08/2026

The global shipping system in mid-2026 is caught between two opposing forces: a front-loading demand surge now rapidly deflating (August US container volumes forecast to fall -4.2% YoY) and structurally elevated freight rates that refuse to follow demand lower — because the supply-side remains fractured by geopolitics, geography, and canal chokepoints. It’s a market where shippers pay more to ship less.

The Demand Cliff After the RushThe big story of H1 2026 was a historic front-loading wave — importers racing to beat tariff changes. July US container imports hit 2.5M TEUs, the fourth-highest July on record, with Chinese-origin shipments rising to 873K TEUs — the highest monthly volume in a year.

That wave has crested. The National Retail Federation/Hackett Associates now projects August volumes down -4.2% YoY, with steady monthly declines through the rest of 2026. The trigger was the tariff shuffle: temporary 10% global tariffs expired Jul 23, replaced by new 10%–12.5% levies on 60 economies covering 99% of US imports.

Rates Don’t Follow Demand DownThe uncomfortable math for importers: ocean transport prices are expected to stay elevated despite falling demand. Fuel and canal surcharges are sticky regardless of volume. A.P. Moller-Maersk (MAERSKb)’s Q2 freight rates jumped +22% YoY, prompting DBS to raise its price target to DKK 17,123 while keeping a Hold — acknowledging the windfall but flagging 2027 headwinds from new capacity additions and a potential Red Sea route reopening.

Multiple simultaneous disruptions are forcing vessels onto longer, costlier routes:

The Panama Power StruggleOne of the most consequential stories: CK Hutchison launched $1.5B+ international arbitration against Panama after losing control of the strategic Balboa and Cristobal terminals flanking the Panama Canal. Panama’s Supreme Court ruled against the concession; the government then seized the ports in February 2026. This is also blocking a $19B+ sale of 43 global terminals to a BlackRock-backed consortium — a deal with major structural implications for global port capacity ownership.

The Transshipment Shadow EconomyWashington is quietly furious: the US estimates it’s losing $19B–$26B annually in tariff revenue on Chinese-origin goods transshipped through countries like Vietnam to avoid US duties. Vietnam’s foreign ministry pledged a “constructive” response, but this is a structural cat-and-mouse problem that keeps trade routes artificially distorted.Source: Investing.com
