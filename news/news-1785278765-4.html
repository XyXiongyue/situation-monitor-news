Texas Leads Venezuelan Oil Import Surge Amid Iran War

Tue, 28 Jul 2026 13:52

Imports of Venezuelan crude oil to the U.S. Gulf Coast have surged to their highest level in eight years, giving American refiners a critical source of heavy crude as the Iran war disrupts shipments from the Middle East.

Average U.S. imports from Venezuela climbed from about 110,000 barrels per day in January to roughly 575,000 barrels per day in June, according tofederal energy data.

That was the highest average daily volume since 2018, the year Nicolas Maduro claimed a disputed reelection that helped trigger U.S. sanctions, effectively halting Venezuelan oil imports beginning in 2019.

The trade resumed after U.S. forces captured Maduro in January and the Trump administration relaxed sanctions on Venezuela's state-owned oil company, Petroleos de Venezuela, or PDVSA.

Texas has received 43% of Venezuela's U.S.-bound crude this year, with shipments flowing to more than 10 refineries designed to process the country's heavy, sulfur-rich oil,The Texas Tribunereported.

Louisiana received another 39%, while the remainder went to Mississippi and Delaware.

The surge has helped compensate for steep declines in shipments from Saudi Arabia and Iraq, which together supplied more than 12% of Gulf Coast crude imports in 2025.

Middle Eastern volumes have nearly disappeared amid restrictions and repeated disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz, the strategic waterway connecting the Persian Gulf with the Arabian Sea.

"It's not shocking that the U.S. is importing a lot of Venezuelan barrels, because they're needed — especially as the strait is shut down," said Debnil Chowdhury, a refining analyst at S&P Global Energy. "You need whatever barrel of heavy crude you can get."

Venezuelan imports have nearly quadrupled since the strait first closed in late February, though analysts expect the rapid growth to slow as the country approaches its current production limits.

Venezuela is producing about 1 million barrels per day, with output projected to level off near 1.15 million barrels per day in 2027 as Chevron and other international companies expand operations under agreements with the Venezuelan government.

"To get a substantial next wave of production, there's got to be some significant capital investment, which we don't think will really bring significant volume out of the ground until the early 2030s," said Kyle Bertamini, principal analyst at Enverus.

Venezuela once produced more than 3 million barrels per day. But years of underinvestment, economic turmoil, sanctions, and deteriorating infrastructure sharply reduced output.

The country's June 24 earthquakes did not significantly damage its main oil-producing regions, refineries, or export terminals, according toearly industry assessments.

The disaster killed more than 5,000 people and caused an estimated $19.6 billion in damage, raising concerns that reconstruction could divert resources from energy investment.

"If the government of Venezuela has to redirect funds for earthquake relief, then that could affect some production" of oil, Chowdhury said.

"It is important to realize that now more than ever, Venezuela's recovery will depend on its ability to leverage the country's abundant natural resources and, most importantly, the resilience, talent, and determination of its people," said Lorena Moscardelli, director of the University of Texas Bureau of Economic Geology.
