War in Ukraine big damper on Russia’s energy industry

Fri, 24 Jul 2026 17:00

Each new phase of Russia's war with Ukraine further devolves Russia's energy industry from political setbacks to Ukraine effectively targeting refineries and other critical infrastructure to significant drags on production.

The questions arise if Russian President Vladimir Putin has taken the industry for granted in pursuing the war and if he is wrecking his oil and gas business to the point that it will end up in a state of Venezuela-like devastation.

Waco economist Ray Perryman and Odessa oilman Kirk Edwards say the damages can eventually be repaired, but it will be a long time before Russia's participation in the global oil economy is like it was before the war started in February 2022.

While Russia's energy sector has been damaged, Perryman said, it is not yet on a path comparable to that of Venezuela.

"The longer the conflict continues, the worse the outcome for the industry in Russia will be, but it is very different than Venezuela where the infrastructure suffered from decades of neglect," he said. "Ukraine's attacks on refineries, pipelines and export terminals are generally repairable.

"They have reduced capacity and forced Russia to spend heavily on repairs, but unless a facility is completely destroyed it can be brought back into service."

Perryman said a bigger issue for Russia is likely the cumulative effects of sanctions and isolation over an extended period.

"The modern oil and gas industry depends on advanced technologies, specialized equipment and software and engineering expertise that is rapidly evolving," he said. "Western sanctions have reduced availability of these necessary inputs, which are a major factor in global competitiveness.

"It is difficult to say whether Russia believed energy demand would continue irrespective of geopolitics or whether the objectives of the war outweighed the costs."

He said there were obviously some serious miscalculations on a variety of fronts with the cost and duration far exceeding expectations.

"Nonetheless Russia has a larger and more diversified economy than Venezuela, continues to produce and export millions of barrels of oil per day and has significant engineering and industrial capacity," Perryman said. "While it has had issues it has not seen the level of neglect that occurred in Venezuela.

"These characteristics make it more likely that Russia would face a slow decline than an abrupt collapse."

Edwards said one of the biggest lessons from this war is that neither side fully anticipated how the battlefield would evolve over time.

"As the conflict dragged on Ukraine has become increasingly sophisticated in identifying targets that have the greatest strategic and economic impact," he said. "Early in the war its ability to strike inside Russia was limited.

"Today it has demonstrated that Russia's energy infrastructure, particularly its refineries, fuel storage facilities and related logistics network, are some of the country's greatest vulnerabilities."

For a nation whose economy depends so heavily on oil and gas revenues, Edwards said, these attacks inflict damage that extends well beyond the immediate loss of refining capacity.

"They reduce fuel availability, disrupt exports, increase repair costs and force Russia to divert resources toward protecting critical infrastructure rather than investing elsewhere," he said. "In many respects Ukraine is now striking Russia where it hurts most financially while largely avoiding attacks intended to maximize civilian casualties.

"I believe the Kremlin underestimated these long-term consequences when it launched the invasion."

While military planners often focus on the opening stages of a conflict, Edwards said, they can overlook how an adaptive opponent will eventually identify and exploit critical economic weak points.

"Russia's energy sector has now become one of those weak points," he said. "Can Russia recover? Yes, but not quickly.

"Modern refineries are among the most complex industrial facilities in the world and repairing them requires specialized equipment, advanced engineering expertise and access to sophisticated components."

Under normal circumstances, Edwards said, restoring damaged facilities can take many months.

"Under sanctions with limited access to Western technology and suppliers, those repairs could stretch into years," he said. "I don't believe Russia is necessarily headed toward a Venezuela-style collapse. They still possesses enormous oil and natural gas reserves, a highly educated technical workforce and the financial ability to rebuild over time."

However, there are meaningful parallels, Edwards said.

"Venezuela demonstrated how years of underinvestment, isolation from Western technology and political priorities overriding sound energy policy can steadily erode one of the world's great petroleum industries," he said. "If Russia remains isolated from global capital markets and advanced energy technology for an extended period, the cumulative damage to its energy sector could become increasingly difficult and expensive to reverse.

"Ultimately the greatest casualty may not be Russia's resource base. It still has tremendous hydrocarbons, but the longer the war continues the greater the risk that temporary infrastructure damage becomes a long-term competitive disadvantage for one of the world's largest energy producers."
