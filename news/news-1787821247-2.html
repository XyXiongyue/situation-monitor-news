OPEC+ loses grip on oil market as Iran war shifts influence to China

Thu, 27 Aug 2026 01:48

Alliance's global market share falls to 40% from 48% as Hormuz blockade blunts its power to raise or cut supply

Six months into the Iran war, OPEC+, the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries and allies including Russia, finds itself unable to influence an oil market it once helped shape, with its statements and policy decisions barely moving prices anymore.

The war has shut a major export route for Middle Eastern oil, damaged energy infrastructure across several OPEC countries, and eroded the alliance's market share along with its pricing power.

In its place, sharp cuts in Chinese crude imports have emerged as one of 2026's dominant market forces, helping balance oil supply amid what analysts describe as the worst-ever disruption to global crude flows.

OPEC+accounted for about 40% of global oil output in July, down from more than 48% before the US and Israel attacked Iran in late February, according to Reuters calculations based on International Energy Agency data. Roughly four to five percentage points of that decline stem from the United Arab Emirates' withdrawal from OPEC in May.

The alliance's core group of seven producers, including Saudi Arabia and Russia, accounted for just a quarter of global oil output in July.

By effectively shutting the Strait of Hormuz, a critical export route for Saudi Arabia and fellow members Iraq and Kuwait, the war has stripped OPEC+ of its usual ability to quickly raise or lower supply.

OPEC was founded in 1960, and the wider OPEC+ framework emerged in 2016 when Russia and other producers joined to help counter the group's shrinking share of world output.

OPEC's share of global crude production peaked at around 50% during the oil crises of the 1970s before sliding to 30% by the mid-1980s as output rose from the North Sea, Alaska and Siberia.

OPEC did not respond to a request for comment.

OPEC+ says its decisions are aimed at supporting market stability and that it does not target a specific price.

Wartime supply disruptions are nothing new for OPEC, from Kuwait during the 1990-91 Gulf War to Iraq following the 2003 US-led invasion. What sets the current crisis apart is its scale, constraining multiple producers at once and limiting the group's ability to offset losses elsewhere.

Since March, the core OPEC+ group has announced six output increases, yet most have remained largely theoretical because of the Hormuz blockade, with little real effect on prices — except briefly in July, during a US-Iran ceasefire that raised hopes the strait might reopen.

The contrast with 2019 is stark: back then, OPEC+ and US President Donald Trump, in his first term, frequently clashed over oil prices, and traders closely watched OPEC+ decisions for market impact. At the time, the central question was how much oil OPEC+ chose to pump; now, it's how much oil can physically be produced and exported at all amid the ongoing war.

One of this year's biggest price drivers has been a steep decline in Chinese oil imports, with China buying roughly 400 million fewer barrels since the war began than over the same period last year — a drop tied to a ban on fuel exports, lower refining output, and the growing adoption of electric transport.

The shift underscores China's expanding role in balancing oil markets, a function once associated almost exclusively with OPEC+ as the world's swing producer. Weaker Chinese demand has helped cap prices this year, in sharp contrast to last year's buying spree, which may have driven as much as half of global oil demand growth and helped underpin the market.

"They've become the swing demand centre," said June Goh, an analyst at Sparta Commodities.
