Australian Senate Report Eyes Bolstering Russia Sanctions, Calls Out Lack of Enforcement

Wed, 26 Aug 2026 17:00

The Australian Senate’s Foreign Affairs, Defence and Trade References Committee recommends in a newreportthat its government take a host of steps to strengthen its sanctions against Russia, including that it work with friendly countries to better align, enforce and measure the effectiveness of those designations.

The committee also called on the Australia government to “more promptly” impose sanctions on Russian entities that warrant such designations and “take all reasonable and necessary steps in order to close loopholes” in its sanctions regime.

The panel also would expand sanctions on Russian entities involved in human rights abuses, including wrongful detention; prioritize considering Ukraine's sanctions recommendations; look into sanctioning Russian timber and products derived from Russian oil; and consider giving agencies more funding to investigate, freeze and seize the assets of Russian sanctioned entities.

Russia’s February 2022 invasion of Ukraine led the Australian Government to impose an “unprecedented” 1,800 sanctions against Russia, the report says. But the committee said its “inquiry” into the effectiveness of those measures found room for improvement.

“While submissions received by the committee by and large agreed that Australia’s sanctions against Russia are broadly sound, shortcomings were identified,” the committee wrote. “These include lacking ambition in terms of scale, loopholes which allow circumvention and undermine effectiveness, as well as a perceived absence of enforcement.”

During its inquiry, the committee was told Australia has conducted no Russia-related sanctions prosecutions.

“Given the sheer size and scope of the sanctions …, it is unlikely that this lack of prosecution is due a complete absence of sanctions evasion on Australian soil,” the panel wrote. “A reasonable conclusion is that evasion has either taken place undetected, or that instances have been detected but not prosecuted. It is impossible for the committee to know why the latter might be the case.”

The committee also heard “concerns about compliance monitoring and transparency, as well as the risk of sanctions circumvention through complex asset ownership structures and trade pathways which remain open.”

While Australia has barred direct imports of Russian crude oil, it has increased its purchases of refined petroleum products from India, a major buyer of Russian crude.

“This situation is made possible by a loophole in Australian sanctions policy which allows the importation of products derived from Russian oil if those are refined and exported by a third country,” the report says. “The result is a profound funding contradiction. While Australia supports Ukraine through military and financial aid, we are nonetheless providing Russia with billions in income by importing refined petroleum derived from Russian crude oil.”

The committee said that Russian timber poses a similar problem. While Russian timber is not banned in Australia, it faces a high tariff. To get around the tariff, the timber is refined in third-party countries, especially China, and imported into Australia as engineered wood products, such as laminated timber.
