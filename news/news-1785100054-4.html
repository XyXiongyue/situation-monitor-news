Aging bulkers: Balancing costs and performance – With the right investments, age is just a number

2026-07-27 05:07

inInternational Shipping News27/07/2026

Fleets ageBulk carriers represent about 42% of the global fleet by deadweight tonnage, and these bulkers are aging fast, largely due to slower scrapping and modest ordering. The average vessel age increased from about 8 years old in 2018 to nearly 13 in 2025, with vessels over 16 years old accounting for approximately 29% of the total fleet.

Despite modest ordering over the years, bulk carrier deliveries are expected to reach a six-year high in 2026, with about 41.2 million DWT of deliveries this year. Even with an expected improvement in bulk market conditions in 2026, the high number of new vessel deliveries expected this year could put downward pressure on earnings.

Oil tankers, meanwhile, represent about 28% of the global fleet by deadweight tonnage, with chemical tankers adding an additional 2.2%. The global tanker fleet is also aging fast, with lower scrapping rates, influenced by strong market conditions, and limited fleet renewal driving this trend. The fleet has also never been older, with an average age of over 14 years old and around 53% of tankers over 15.

Tanker orders have declined sharply, down by approximately 55% since 2024, but there is still expected to be a surge of new vessel deliveries over the next few years. The tanker segment has been enjoying a strong period of demand and crude tanker rates remain solid. Tanker owners will understandably try to ride the wave of high earnings as long as possible, before the potential market contraction.

Given this market context, the goal should be unlocking efficiency and high performance without necessarily investing too much in aging vessels. In turn, selecting the right hull coating for the vessel’s unique operating profile, with the right balance of capital expenditure (CAPEX) and performance is key.

Costs expandThere is no doubt that the cost equation is becoming more complex and multifaceted. With the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS), FuelEU Maritime, and the proposed IMO Net-Zero Framework greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions can now essentially be seen as costs, and those costs are set to increase significantly over the coming years.

For bulkers and tankers – using a realistic EU Allowance (EUA) emissions credit price of €80-95 per t/CO2 – the EU ETS can drive annual carbon levies in excess of $2M. While this is influenced by fuel type, consumption levels, EU exposure and trading patterns – the direction of travel is clear. Inefficient fuel consumption translates directly into significant annual operating expenditure (OPEX) exposure.

This is also driven by the fact that fuel often remains ship operators’ largest OPEX. The link between saving fuel, reducing GHG emissions, and saving money is clearer than ever before. Notably, saving fuel can also build resilience against unpredictable fuel prices. This becomes even more apparent with alternative fuels, as green fuel is more expensive, less energy-dense, and a scarcer resource than conventional fuel.

Minimizing off-hire costs, drydock costs, equipment maintenance costs, and hull cleaning costs – all while maintaining good vessel performance – is also part of the challenge. This is where hull, ballast tank, cargo hold and other equipment coatings can be a secret weapon. They represent ‘low-hanging fruit’ that must be picked. So, how can shipowners and operators make informed-decisions?

Coatings matterFirstly, consider that no two ships are the same. Trading patterns, speeds, idle times and routes all mean that what is best for one vessel is not automatically best for another. Not every vessel needs the very top-end coating system. It is more important that solutions match actual trading profiles and financial reality. Tanker and bulker operators should seek the right performance at the right cost without hidden complexity.

For some ships, ultra-premium products are the best fit, but for others these may create challenges. The upfront investment of top-tier systems doesn’t always fit drydock or fleet budgets. If application processes are complex, there may be extra lifecycle costs, for example if schemes require higher film build, specialised equipment, or full re-blasts at each docking. Finally, if the highest-end product is not needed, some owners may end up paying for performance their vessels never fully use.
