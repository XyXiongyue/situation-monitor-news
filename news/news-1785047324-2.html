Will China’s oil appetite be enough to support prices?

Sat, 25 Jul 2026 23:02

Investing.com --China's sharp reduction in crude imports has helped contain the rise in global oil prices, but shrinking inventories elsewhere could still send crude to $120 a barrel or higher if the Strait of Hormuz remains closed, Capital Economics said.

Chinese crude imports fell more than 40% year over year in June to 7.2 million barrels per day. That was more than 4 million barrels per day below pre-war levels, equal to roughly 30% of the crude that passed through Hormuz before the Iran conflict.

The decline was much larger than those recorded during the pandemic. Analysts said it primarily reflected a shift from stockpiling to drawing down inventories, rather than a sudden collapse in underlying oil demand.

China accumulated an estimated 700,000 to 1.1 million barrels per day of surplus crude during 2025. Port inventories have since fallen sharply as refiners and policymakers used those reserves to limit purchases during the supply shock.

Falling end-user demand played a smaller role. China's oil consumption declined by about 5% from a year earlier in June, according to US Energy Information Administration estimates.

The rapid adoption of electric vehicles is a longer-term pressure on fuel consumption, but analysts said it could not explain such a large change within a few months. Lower petrochemical activity, coal substitution and a temporary refined-fuel export ban also had limited effects.

China held an estimated 1.4 billion barrels of commercial and strategic crude reserves at the end of 2025. That represented around 120 days of pre-war import coverage and was 70% above comparable US reserves.

Those holdings could let China maintain imports near current levels for several more months, possibly into 2027. Running inventories lower would leave the country less protected against another supply disruption.

Chinese buying is unlikely to recover to pre-war levels before oil returns toward $60 a barrel and stockpiling becomes attractive again.

China's reduced purchases may not shield the wider market indefinitely. OECD commercial inventories have continued falling since the Iran conflict began, pointing to tighter supply conditions if Hormuz remains blocked for an extended period.
