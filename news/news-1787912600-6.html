Why a long Iran conflict could keep Hormuz shipping risky and costs high

2026-08-28 18:23

inInternational Shipping News28/08/2026

Markets may be hoping for a quick way out of the Iran conflict, but one of the shipping industry’s leading figures believes they could be badly misreading the situation.

According to the Financial Times, Jacob Meldgaard, chief executive of Danish tanker group Torm, has warned that the war could settle into a prolonged stalemate resembling the drawn-out conflict between Russia and Ukraine.

Meldgaard believes a deadlock lasting months or even years is more likely than a rapid resolution, despite the heavy economic costs already created by the conflict.

Markets bet on a breakthroughOil prices have fallen this week amid talks between Iran and Oman aimed at restarting shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. The discussions have fuelled hopes that one of the world’s most important energy routes could return to normal. At the same time, however, the US has continued to threaten Tehran with tougher sanctions. The mixed signals underline the uncertainty surrounding the conflict. No easy way outMeldgaard said neither Washington nor Tehran appeared to have an obvious alternative to continuing the confrontation. The US administration, he suggested, has few clear options beyond maintaining pressure, while Iran and its powerful Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps are also unlikely to simply step back. That leaves the Gulf facing the prospect of preparing for a crisis that could last far longer than initially expected.

The region may have to adjust to a new reality rather than wait for conditions to quickly return to how they were before the conflict.

Shipping profits surgeThe disruption has also transformed the economics of tanker shipping. Torm, which operates nearly 100 vessels carrying petrol, diesel and other refined fuels, reported a record net profit of $338 million in the second quarter. That was almost six times higher than its profit during the same period a year earlier, as freight rates surged during the conflict.

The sharp increase highlights how the war has created winners as well as major risks across the energy and shipping industries.

Gulf states build their fleetsGulf countries are also responding to the changing situation by expanding their tanker fleets. The move is aimed at helping national oil companies continue transporting crude and fuel through the Strait of Hormuz despite the threat of Iranian attacks.

For private shipping companies, however, the risks remain deeply personal. Meldgaard said the safety of seafarers remained a serious concern, even as national oil companies increasingly view keeping energy supplies moving as a strategic necessity. The result is a conflict in which economic disruption alone may not be enough to force a breakthrough. And if Meldgaard’s assessment proves correct, markets may have to prepare for something far more difficult than a short-lived crisis: a new and prolonged geopolitical stalemate at the heart of the global energy trade.Source: Moneycontrol
