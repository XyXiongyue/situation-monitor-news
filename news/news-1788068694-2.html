‘Venezuela retains ownership and sovereignty’: Rodriguez backs US oil deal

Sat, 29 Aug 2026 22:30

Venezuela's interim leader says the country will retain ownership of its oil resources under a new agreement giving the US significant access to its vast reserves and attracting $100 billion in proposed private investment

Venezuela's interim leader Delcy Rodriguez sought to reassure the public on Saturday that the country would retain control of its oil resources despite a new agreement giving the United States significant access to its petroleum reserves.

The United States will have majority control of Venezuela's 65 billion barrels of proven reserves in a dealPresident Donald Trump describedon Friday as "the biggest oil deal in world history".

"One thing must be absolutely clear: Venezuela retains ownership and sovereignty over its resources," Rodriguez said in a speech broadcast on state television.

She said the agreement with the United States would transform "cold, inert" resources underground into "a source of social and economic well-being for the people of Venezuela".

Caracas says the agreement involves $100 billion in private investment to revive the oil industry in a country with the world's largest proven oil reserves.

The lack of transparency surrounding the deal, however, has fuelled concerns among Venezuelans, including government supporters. Social media comments have criticised what some described as a loss of sovereignty, limited benefits and political costs.

"We don't know who this will benefit -- whether it's Venezuela or (the United States)," said Jesus Salazar, a 68-year-old security guard.

"I have my doubts, but we'll have to wait and see what happens."

Venezuela's government has faced intense pressure from the Trump administration since American forces ousted and captured long-time ruler Nicolas Maduro in January.

Washington allowed Rodriguez, Maduro's vice president, to remain as interim leader on the condition that she follows Washington's line.

Venezuela's ruling party said on Saturday that it supported economic measures that "prioritise national interests and make it possible to overcome the impact of more than a decade of economic sanctions, unilateral coercive measures, and unjust blockades."

The party has consistently blamed US sanctions for Venezuela's economic collapse.

Rodriguez has introduced reforms in Venezuela's mining and petroleum industries, opening sectors that were previously tightly controlled to private capital and foreign investment.

Washington has also eased sanctions imposed on Venezuela's oil sector under the Maduro government.

Oil production increased by 29.8 per cent between January and July, reaching 1.2 million barrels per day. That remains well below the three million barrels per day produced a quarter of a century ago.

The new US deal seeks to restore production to those levels, although analysts say that would take time.

"The increase in production won't be seen for at least three or four years," said engineer Oswaldo Felizzola, a professor at the Institute of Higher Studies in Administration (IESA) in Caracas.

Felizzola welcomed the United States acting as a "guarantor" for investment in Venezuela, which has struggled to attract significant capital for more than a decade.

"Without this, these fields would not be developed over the next 10 or 15 years," because state-owned Petroleos de Venezuela (PDVSA) "does not have the financial resources to do so."

Oil deal comes amid deep economic hardship

An initiative of this scale could accelerate Venezuela's long-awaited economic recovery after the country suffered an 80 per cent contraction between 2014 and 2021, pushing millions of Venezuelans into poverty.

Many people survive on a monthly minimum wage equivalent to $0.16, supplemented by government subsidies that can reach $240 per month. Even together, these remain far below the estimated $730 required to meet the basic food needs of a family of five.

Some Venezuelans remain uncertain about what the agreement will mean for their daily lives.

Carlos Baco, a 74-year-old government employee, hopes oil revenues will begin flowing under the new deal but said he has yet to see the benefits of increased production in recent months.

"Consumer goods and food are more expensive... the dollar is rising and prices are going up," he said. "It's impossible here."

Rodriguez has promised more than $204 billion in tax revenue from the agreement but has not provided details.

For analyst Elias Ferrer of the Orinoco think tank, the investment represents positive news even without further specifics.

"It's undoubtedly better than before. Before, this oil wasn't being extracted; if no one extracts it, no one pays royalties, and no one profits from it," he said.

"If everything is done correctly... this will be a great opportunity for Venezuela to see its oil sector revived," said attorney Dolores Dobarro, Venezuela's former deputy minister of energy.
