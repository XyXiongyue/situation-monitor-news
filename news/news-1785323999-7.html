Kazakhstan’s Oil Exports Face Black Sea Chokepoint

Wed, 29 Jul 2026 04:10

The Caspian Pipeline Consortium, which exportsaround 80% of Kazakhstan’s oil exports, was turned off for a week in mid-July, as producers grappled with the consequences of Russia and Ukraine’s heated conflict in the Black Sea.

BetweenJuly 17-19, Ukrainian drones hit multiple oil tankers sailing to and from the CPC Marine Terminal at the Russian port of Novorossiysk, where the CPC ends its 1,500 km-journey originating from Kazakhstan’s largest oil fields, located in the west of the country.

The attacks drew harshcondemnationfrom Kazakhstan’s government, but were more significant as they led to the wholesale suspension of the CPC’s operations – a move that the pipeline’s management attributed to an act of “terrorism,” though they did not name a specific country or actor at fault.

Despite oil flows resuming this week, experts surveyed by Vlast said that the attacks have cast doubt on the CPC’s viability amid Ukraine’sincreasingly intense drone campaign, and raised major questions for key players in the global energy industry, while threatening a key source of Kazakhstan’s budget revenues.

The events of last week showed that Ukraine’s drone campaign has the potential to severely disrupt oil routes spanning the Black and Mediterranean Seas.

Francesco Sassi, a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Oslo and the author of theEnergy Geopolitics & Statecraftblog, said the attacks have put diplomats, producers, and traders on alert.

“The situation is not going well neither for Kazakhstan’s government nor for the oil producers,” Sassi told Vlast.

According to Natalia Katona, an independent oil analyst based in Abu Dhabi, continued attacks could lead to a dramatic change to the regional energy landscape.

“Buyers of Black Sea oil have become reluctant to renew previous purchase orders because of the recent direct attacks on tankers,” Katona told Vlast.

Most of the oil loaded at Novorossiysk originates from Kazakhstan and is shipped by international oil companies operating at the three major fields of Tengiz, Kashagan, and Karachaganak.

Notably, the Black Sea strikes prompted a temporary dip in production at the Tengiz oil field, Kazakhstan’s primary source of oil exports. Tengizchevroil, which operates the field, is a joint venture between the US’s Chevron and Exxon, Kazakhstan’s Kazmunaigas, and Russia’s Lukoil. The company is also, by far, Kazakhstan’s top taxpayer.

Chevron also owns a 15% stake in the CPC, which makes it a key player in the movement of oil from Kazakhstan to its export markets.

But now, despite efforts by Mike Wirth, the company’s CEO, toreportedlylobby the US government into pressuring Ukraine away from attacking its Black Sea-assets, Kyiv’s strikes have put the CPC’s viability in jeopardy, at least in the short term.

“It’s important to remember that CPC is an international consortium. The fact that the terminal was hit puts all producers, including the Western companies working in Kazakhstan’s upstream, in danger,” Sassi told Vlast.

Chevron seems to be the company most eager to resume shipments, despite fears from ship captains.

“Vessels [in the Black Sea] are contracted by companies like Chevron. This means that it is also up to the captains and the insurers of the ships to decide whether it’s safe to make the trip. After the attacks, the captains have become more pessimistic about the route,” Katona said.

During the CPC shutdown, producers in Kazakhstan halved oil output from 2.1 to 1 million barrels a day.

“It’s an unfolding situation and, while it’s not possible to just flick a switch and turn supplies on and off, it’s important to gauge the feeling of the companies involved in the transport of oil through the Black Sea into the Mediterranean,” Katona said.

Under normal conditions, the CPC handles 1.7 million barrels a day. According to industry data seen by Vlast, around 1.4 million barrels (82%) are delivered to Europe. A smaller portion goes to Asian markets, while the Russian component of the CPC Blend goes exclusively to Indian customers.

“The European customers include mostly Italy and the Netherlands, althoughthree quarters of the shipmentsthat reach the Italian port of Trieste are then loaded onto the Trans-Alpine Pipeline (TAL) and end up in Austrian, German, and Czech refineries,” Katona said.

Kplerdata reviewed by Vlast showed that, in June 2026, the 330,700 barrels a day originating from CPC and shipped via TAL represented 40% of the pipeline’s total capacity.

In July, following the attacks, shipments to Trieste, where TAL begins, fell to 195,500 barrels a day. This is in line with previous slumps in connection with attacks carried out inprevious years.

Because oil production, transit, and sales slumped in Kazakhstan last week, the state budget will likely end up seeing reduced contributions by oil producers at year’s end. According to estimates byone local analyst, the Government effectively lost $32 million each day during the shutdown.

After a week-long pause in operations at Novorossiysk’s CPC Marine Terminal, since July 27 at least three Chevron-contracted tankers have beenservicedthere.

At the time of writing, two tankers, the Seamajesty and the Milos, are being loaded at the port, while the Asia was loaded on July 27 and has already set sail. According to Katona, these tankers are loading Chevron-produced oil.

The three tankers areSuezmax-class vessels, which can hold up to one million barrels of oil. This means that at least one vessel per day now needs to be loaded with oil already pumped to sea terminals at Novorossiysk, without any further disruptions, to avoid a surplus accruing at the port’s storage facilities.

Besides security issues, the volume of oil flows through the CPC in the future could also be affected by the ongoing arbitration dispute against the operators of the Kashagan field, another major contributor to the pipeline.

“Kashagan produces 400,000 barrels a day and its oil is crucial to produce the CPC Blend,” Katona said, referring to the product comprising oil from Kazakhstan’s various oil fields

Kazakhstan’s government and NCOC, the consortium of international companies operating Kashagan, are currently embroiled in anacrimonious arbitrationover lost profit and miscalculated costs relating to the two sides’ production sharing agreement. Kazakhstan’s government has said it wants $166 billion as compensation.

The companies are also facing a 2.3 trillion tenge ($4.9 billion)environmental fine. Kazmunaigas, the state oil and gas company that owns 16.88% in NCOC, said it is ready to pay its part. The rest of the NCOC consortium ispushing backany accusation of wrongdoing.

“International oil companies are reluctant to put in any more investment. They’re shying away not only because of security at the Black Sea terminal. They are also looking at what Kazakhstan’s government is pursuing via legal channels. The arbitration is setting the country up for aworse business environment,” Katona said.
